# Psychology and Emotional Design of UI for a Tool Used Under Stress (DashLens)

Scope note: DashLens is a local, free, browser-based dashcam viewer. Primary user: a stressed, hurried, possibly first-time car owner right after an incident who must find the moment, understand it and save proof. Secondary user: an enthusiast who reviews drives often. Privacy promise: "nothing is uploaded, nothing is tracked."

Method note: in this session, WebFetch to nngroup.com, credibility.stanford.edu and uxpajournal.org was blocked by the network sandbox (DNS failure). Findings therefore come from search-result abstracts and summaries, preferring primary sources (journal pages, author pages, official guides) where the search surfaced them. Items I could not verify are listed under Gaps rather than stated as fact.

Evidence grading used below: **[Strong]** = peer-reviewed, replicated or meta-analysed; **[Moderate]** = peer-reviewed but limited replication, or authoritative practitioner research; **[Weak/Popular]** = practitioner heuristics, single small studies, or claims that have failed replication.

## 1. Aesthetic-usability effect, halo effect, first impressions, perceived quality

### Takeaway
Attractive interfaces are reliably *perceived* as more usable and more credible, and that impression forms in about 50 ms and can persist after use [Moderate-Strong]; but aesthetics does not reliably improve *actual* task performance, and when usability is clearly bad, users notice [Moderate]. For DashLens: polish buys a first-use benefit of the doubt (critical for a stressed first-timer), but it must not hide a broken workflow, and it must never be used to make evidence look more certain than it is.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin (Kurosu & Kashimura 1995) and replication:** Kurosu and Kashimura found, with Japanese participants, that apparent (pre-use) usability ratings of ATM layouts correlated strongly with aesthetic ratings. Tractinsky (1997) replicated this in Israel; the authors report the replication mostly improved the method and showed the original findings were robust, with differences in the size of correlations between the Japanese and Israeli samples (not in the predicted cross-cultural direction). [Moderate] — [Tractinsky publication page, Ben-Gurion University](https://www.bgu.ac.il/en/researcher/noam-tractinsky/publications/351759634/)
- **"What is beautiful is usable" (Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar 2000, *Interacting with Computers* 13(2):127):** the strong correlation between perceived aesthetics and perceived usability stayed intact *after* participants used the system; post-use perceptions were affected by aesthetics, and not by the actual (manipulated) usability. [Moderate] — [Interacting with Computers abstract](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article-abstract/13/2/127/898608); [PDF via Aalto course page](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf)
- **Limits / boundary conditions:** a later study, "Exploring the Boundary Conditions of the Effect of Aesthetics on Perceived Usability" (Journal of Usability Studies, UXPA), failed to show the hypothesized effect of aesthetics on usability judgments; SUS ratings tracked the manipulated usability instead. [Moderate] — [UXPA Journal of Usability Studies](https://uxpajournal.org/?p=4392)
- **Effect on performance is mixed:** search summaries of a review-style paper report that the few studies on whether visual appeal changes *performance* give mixed support; some found no performance effect, and one found aesthetics helped only on low-usability websites (attribution taken from a search summary; full text not read). [Moderate, unverified detail] — [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4577531)
- **50 ms first impression (Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown 2006, *Behaviour & Information Technology*):** participants rated the visual appeal of web pages flashed for 50 ms on a 1–100 scale; 50 ms ratings correlated well with ratings made after longer viewing; the paper concludes designers have about 50 ms to make a good first impression. Lindgaard reportedly said colleagues had thought nothing could be seen in under 500 ms. [Moderate-Strong; widely replicated in follow-ups] — [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01449290500330448); [Nature news, 13 Jan 2006](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html)
- **What drives the 50 ms impression:** follow-up research hosted by Google Research reports that first-impression judgments depend on factors such as visual complexity and prototypicality (how much the page looks like what users expect for its category); low complexity and high prototypicality were rated most appealing (paper appears to be Tuch et al. 2012; author attribution not verified in this session). [Moderate] — [Google Research PDF](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **Popular coverage overstates the finding:** search summaries note that some press coverage goes further than the paper (e.g., treating 50 ms as a judgment of trustworthiness or usability, when the study measured visual appeal). [Note] — [Digital Trends coverage](https://www.digitaltrends.com/home/surfers-judge-web-pages-seen-for-only-50ms/); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/39327/the-50-millisecond-judgment.html)
- **Norman's thesis "attractive things work better":** Norman argues that positive affect broadens thinking and makes people more tolerant of minor difficulties, while negative affect narrows focus. This is presented as Norman's thesis, supported by some affect research, not as a directly tested effect of interface attractiveness under stress. [Weak-Moderate as applied to UI] — [Interaction Design Foundation summary](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-emotions-impact-cognition); [LinkedIn Learning/Lynda course](https://www.lynda.com/Web-Design-tutorials/Attractive-things-work-better/639071/702076-4.html)

### Inferences
- **Implication: make the first screen visually calm and expected.** The stressed first-timer forms a judgment before reading anything. Because prototypicality and low visual complexity drive good first impressions, the drop zone should look like a familiar media player or file-open screen: one clear title, one primary action ("Open dashcam folder or video"), a short privacy line, a lot of white space. Do not show a dense telemetry dashboard to someone who has not loaded a file yet.
- **Implication: use polish to buy patience, not to hide defects.** Tractinsky 2000 says looks colour post-use judgments, but the boundary-conditions study says obvious usability problems override looks. The core flow (open, find event, export clip) must work before visual refinement. A beautiful viewer that fails on a common camera file format will lose trust faster, because the polish set higher expectations.
- **Implication: never let aesthetics overstate evidence.** For a forensic-adjacent tool, the "halo" can work against the user: smoothed GPS tracks, interpolated speeds or a stylised map can look more precise than the data. Show raw sample points or a "GPS signal lost" state where data is missing; show units and sample rate. Credibility with an insurer depends on the proof being honest, not pretty.
- **Implication for the enthusiast:** first impressions matter less for repeat users. Their perceived quality comes from behaviour (speed, keyboard shortcuts, precise scrubbing), which links to Norman's behavioral level (section 5).

### Gaps
- Could not read the full boundary-conditions paper (uxpajournal.org was blocked), so its sample, its manipulation and the size of the null effect are not recorded here.
- No study found that tests the aesthetic-usability effect for users under acute stress, or for video/telemetry tools specifically.
- Other well-known qualifying studies (e.g., Hassenzahl 2004 on beauty vs. goodness; Sonderegger & Sauer 2010 on aesthetics and performance with mobile phones) were not retrieved in this session and are not cited.

## 2. Trust and credibility signals in software; privacy reassurance; transparency; no dark patterns

### Takeaway
Ordinary users judge credibility largely on look and feel; in the Stanford study, design was the most-cited factor [Moderate-Strong]. For a privacy-first local tool, trust comes from a design that fits the purpose plus *verifiable* transparency: say what the app did, offer proof that nothing left the device, and use no manipulative patterns. Survivor-led design guidance (Chayn) explicitly ranks trust and safety above aesthetics or simplicity.

### Cited Findings
- **Stanford Web Credibility guidelines (Fogg et al.):** ten guidelines drawn from three years of research with more than 4,500 people. One design guideline says the visual design should match the site's purpose. Other guidelines widely reproduced from this list include making it easy to verify information, showing a real organisation behind the site, making the site easy to use and useful, updating content often, being restrained with promotional content, and avoiding errors of all types, "no matter how small they seem" (full list not re-read this session because the page was blocked). [Moderate] — [Stanford Persuasive Tech Lab guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines); [Wikipedia: Stanford Web Credibility Project](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Web_Credibility_Project)
- **Stanford 2002 large study ("How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility?"):** 2,684 ordinary participants rated sites in ten content areas; 46.1% of comments cited overall design or "look" as a basis for credibility, the most frequent category. Fogg: "To look good is to be good — that's the primary test when people assess a Web site's credibility." Sources disagree on the date (May vs. October 2002). [Moderate] — [InternetNews coverage](https://www.internetnews.com/marketing/study-for-site-cred-looks-matter); [Writers Write coverage](https://www.writerswrite.com/good-design-enhances-website-credibility-11202002388)
- **Experts differ from consumers:** a companion study compared expert and ordinary evaluators; consumers rated finance sites relying even more heavily on design look. [Moderate] — [Consumer Reports WebWatch PDF](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/expert-vs-online-consumers.pdf)
- **Prominence-interpretation theory (Fogg):** credibility is judged from elements users *notice* (prominence) and how they *interpret* them; an unnoticed trust signal has no effect. [Moderate] — [NN/g article on prominence-interpretation theory](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prominence-interpretation-theory/)
- **Dark patterns are common and detectable:** Mathur et al. (CSCW 2019) crawled ~53K product pages on ~11K shopping sites and found 1,818 dark-pattern instances (15 types, 7 categories) on 1,254 sites (~11.1%); 183 sites used outright deceptive practices such as countdown timers that reset on reload and fake testimonials; 22 third-party vendors sold dark patterns as plug-ins. The authors say this is likely a low estimate. [Strong] — [arXiv 1907.07032](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07032v2); [Princeton project page](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns); [UChicago news](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/dirty-tricks-online-shopping-designed-make-you-spend-more)
- **Trauma-informed design (Chayn):** Chayn's eight principles are safety, agency, equity, privacy, accountability, plurality, sharing power and hope. Chayn says it "prioritise[s] trust and safety over aesthetic or simplicity," and describes the principles as provisional and still changing. Hera Hussain's earlier version listed nine principles focused on safety and avoiding re-traumatisation. [Practitioner guidance; Moderate for domain] — [Catalyst/Promo Cymru introduction](https://promo.cymru/resource/trauma-informed-design-an-introduction-for-non-profits); [Hera Hussain, Medium](https://medium.com/orbiting/stopping-abuse-design-principles-for-creating-trauma-informed-technology-for-tackling-ed12bef494); [UX Magazine series](https://uxmag.com/articles/trauma-informed-design-understanding-trauma-and-healing)
- **Consistency as safety (Chayn's Bloom):** in practice, this means predictable timing and consistent messages throughout a programme. [Practitioner] — [Chayn blog, user research](https://blog.chayn.co/tagged/user-research)

### Inferences
- **Implication: make the privacy promise prominent and checkable.** Under prominence-interpretation theory, a privacy line hidden in a footer does nothing. Put one short sentence by the open-file control ("Your videos stay on this computer. Nothing is uploaded.") and, after loading, a quiet status such as "Processed on this device · 0 network requests." Offer an "How to check" link explaining how to confirm with the browser's network tab, or that the app works offline. Being verifiable is what separates a credible claim from marketing.
- **Implication: be open about third-party calls.** If map tiles or fonts come from a network, the "nothing is uploaded" claim needs a precise qualifier ("Map tiles are downloaded from X; your location and videos are never sent") and a switch to turn the map off. One undisclosed network call found by a technical user would break the promise for everyone (the Stanford rule to avoid errors of all types applies to trust claims too).
- **Implication: say what the app did.** After loading: "Loaded 14 clips (front + rear), 2 h 06 min, 3 events from the camera's G-sensor, GPS for 97% of the time." After export: file name, duration, time range, the fact that the original file was not changed, and where the file was saved. Clear accounts of what happened carry Chayn's "accountability" principle and also reduce anxiety (section 3).
- **Implication: no dark patterns, ever.** No fake urgency, no nagging donation modals during the incident flow, no pre-ticked "share" options, no confirmshaming. For a free tool, a quiet "Support this project" link after the task is done is acceptable; interrupting a stressed user is not.
- **Implication: design that fits the purpose.** Stanford's "match the purpose" guideline suggests a sober, instrument-like look (neutral palette, legible numbers, restrained motion) over a playful consumer-app or "hacker HUD" look. For proof meant for insurers and police, the export should look like a document: timestamp, source file name, camera model if known, and a note on what was not changed.

### Gaps
- Full text of the Stanford guidelines and the 2002 study could not be fetched (credibility.stanford.edu was blocked); the list above relies on secondary sources and should be checked against the original.
- No empirical study found on which privacy-reassurance wordings measurably increase trust in local-only web apps.
- No sourced guidance found on evidence-integrity cues (for example, hashing exported clips) that insurers or police actually value; this belongs to another research thread.

## 3. Designing for stressed users: cognitive load, Hick's law, progressive disclosure, calm tone, error prevention, reassurance

### Takeaway
Acute stress measurably impairs working memory and cognitive flexibility [Strong, meta-analysis], so the incident user has less mental capacity for exactly the things a complex viewer demands. Reduce choices at each step (Hick's law), hide advanced features until needed (progressive disclosure), make every action reversible, and write plain, specific, blame-free messages (GOV.UK style).

### Cited Findings
- **Stress impairs core executive functions:** Shields, Sazma & Yonelinas (2016) meta-analysis: acute stress impaired working memory and cognitive flexibility, with nuanced or mixed effects on inhibition. [Strong] — [PubMed 27371161](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27371161/); [PMC5003767](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003767)
- **Stress effects on decisions depend on context:** a 2024 review in *Brain, Behavior, & Immunity – Health* proposes that timing between stressor and task, stressor type, decision context and individual characteristics shape the outcome; stress can even enhance some memory encoding. [Moderate] — [PMC11407068](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11407068/)
- **Anxiety narrows attention:** a review on anxiety and perception reports anxiety predicts a tendency to "miss the forest for the trees"; research on selective attention under stress is mixed in its theoretical predictions. [Moderate] — [PMC2933078](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933078); [PubMed 12916567](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12916567/)
- **Hick's law (Hick–Hyman):** decision time rises with the logarithm of the number of equally likely choices, RT = a + b·log2(n). It applies best to simple choice-reaction tasks among equally likely options; designers extend it to justify limiting options. [Strong as lab law; Moderate as UI heuristic] — [LogRocket on Hick's law](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/using-hicks-law-help-users-make-decisions); [UK DfE accessibility guidance, Laws of UX](https://accessibility.education.gov.uk/profession-guidance/interaction-design/laws-of-ux)
- **Progressive disclosure (NN/g, Nielsen):** first show users only a few of the most important options, and offer the rest on request; it helps novices avoid mistakes; if a design needs three or more levels of disclosure, simplify it. [Moderate, authoritative practitioner] — [NN/g: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- **Shneiderman's golden rules:** "permit easy reversal of actions" (it relieves anxiety, because users know errors can be undone) and "support internal locus of control" (users want to feel in charge and to start actions, with the system responding). [Moderate, canonical HCI] — [Interaction Design Foundation](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/shneiderman-s-eight-golden-rules-will-help-you-design-better-interfaces); [UMD course page](https://www.cs.umd.edu/class/fall2002/cmsc838s/tichi/prescriptive.html)
- **GOV.UK error-message guidance (as summarised by UK government services):** say what went wrong and how to fix it; lead with what the user must do; avoid "please" (it implies a choice) and "sorry"; avoid "valid/invalid" (they do not say what is wrong); keep wording consistent with the question; do not just repeat the hint text. [Moderate, government standard] — [GOV.UK Design System: Error message](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message); [GOV.UK Service Manual: Writing for user interfaces](https://gov.uk/service-manual/design/writing-for-user-interfaces); [National Archives design guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/design-guide/forms/checkboxes/); [DfE design history](https://design-histories.education.gov.uk/record-concerns-and-support-for-trusts/making-error-messages-more-consistent); [UK Parliament design system](https://designsystem.parliament.uk/how-tos/writing-error-messages/)
- **Trauma-informed principles relevant here:** safety, agency, privacy, accountability and hope (Chayn). [Practitioner] — [Catalyst/Promo Cymru](https://promo.cymru/resource/trauma-informed-design-an-introduction-for-non-profits)

### Inferences
- **Implication: one obvious path for the incident.** Offer a short "Incident" path: (1) Open files → (2) Jump to the event (the G-sensor spike, or "pick the time it happened") → (3) Review front and rear → (4) Save clip and summary. Show the step the user is on. Keep each screen to one or two primary actions; everything else (overlays, charts, playback speeds, map styles) sits behind "More" or a side panel.
- **Implication: do the work of remembering for the user.** Because working memory is impaired, show the current absolute date and time and speed next to the video at all times, plus a persistent "Marked: 14:32:08–14:32:38" chip once the user marks a range, so they never have to hold numbers in their head.
- **Implication: give strong guesses with easy overrides.** Auto-jump to the strongest G-sensor event or locked/"event" files (cameras often store them in an EVENT folder), but say why ("Jumped to the strongest impact detected at 14:32:11") and offer "Not this one? See all 3 events."
- **Implication: make actions reversible and never destructive.** Reading only, never changing source files; say so. Exports are new files. Marking a range can be undone. No "Are you sure?" dialogs needed when nothing is destroyed; confirmation should be rare and reserved for real loss.
- **Implication: errors that are calm, specific and free of blame**, in GOV.UK style. Instead of "Invalid file," write "This file isn't a video DashLens can read (.NMEA). Choose the .MP4 files from the same folder; the GPS data is inside them." For a partly damaged file: "The last 4 seconds of this clip are damaged, which is common when power is cut in a crash. The rest plays normally." That last sentence also reassures (it gives hope and it explains the cause).
- **Implication: avoid alarm visuals for normal states.** Red banners or flashing icons for routine conditions (no GPS, missing rear camera) raise arousal in an already stressed user. Keep strong warning styling for real data loss.
- **Implication for the enthusiast:** progressive disclosure should not punish experts: keyboard shortcuts, a remembered "expert layout," and direct access to the telemetry panel keep the simple first path from slowing repeat users.

### Gaps
- No empirical HCI study found on interface use specifically by people just after a road collision; the stress literature is general (lab stressors such as the Trier Social Stress Test).
- GOV.UK pages were not fetched directly; the wording rules come from UK government departments' summaries of GOV.UK guidance.
- Hick's law evidence for complex real-world menus (as opposed to choice-reaction tasks) was not examined; treat it as a useful heuristic, not a precise prediction.

## 4. Perceived performance and waiting psychology

### Takeaway
How long a wait *feels* depends on uncertainty, anxiety, being occupied and being informed (Maister) [Moderate, service-research classic]. Response-time thresholds (Nielsen's 0.1/1/10 s; Doherty's 400 ms) are useful engineering targets, but the 400 ms number is a practical target, not a cognitive constant. Evidence that skeleton screens beat spinners is weak and conflicting.

### Cited Findings
- **Maister, "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" (1985):** eight propositions, including: occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time; people want to get started; anxiety makes waits seem longer; uncertain waits feel longer than known, finite waits; unexplained waits feel longer than explained waits. Anxiety is higher while waiting to be served than while being served, and is linked to a fear of being forgotten. If told 30 minutes and it takes 25, people feel lucky; if 35, cheated. [Moderate; widely taught, conceptual rather than experimental] — [Utah Education Network, Services Management, ch. 12](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/servicesmgt/chapter/chapter-12-the-psychology-of-waiting/); [Managing the Psychology of Waiting](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/opexintro/chapter/managing-the-psychology-of-waiting); [David Maister's site](https://davidmaister.com/?p=747)
- **Doherty threshold:** Doherty & Thadani (IBM Systems Journal, 1982, "The Economic Value of Rapid Response Time") argued for a 400 ms system response target instead of the older 2-second standard; Laws of UX: productivity soars when neither the computer nor the user has to wait on the other (<400 ms). Kent Beck argues no research shows attention slipping at exactly 400 ms; it is a practical target, not a universal constant. [Moderate for direction; Weak for the exact number] — [Laws of UX: Doherty Threshold](https://lawsofux.com/doherty-threshold/); [Dave Rupert](https://daverupert.com/2015/06/doherty-threshold/); [Kent Beck, "The Precious Eyeblink"](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/the-precious-eyeblink?open=false)
- **Two clocks:** commentary on slow AI responses suggests separating the time to *acknowledge* an action from the time to *finish* it. [Weak/practitioner] — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/stop-blaming-the-model-for-slow-ai-heres-how-to-design-for-it-doherty-s-threshold-as-a-guideline-5fa6d52e23fc?gi=152943387dd2)
- **NN/g on loading indicators:** skeleton screens can make waits feel shorter by showing the structure being loaded; spinners give no duration and suit waits of about 2–10 s; loads under ~1 s need no indicator. [Moderate, practitioner] — [NN/g: Skeleton Screens 101](https://nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/skeleton-screens-vs-progress-bars-vs-spinners/)
- **Conflicting skeleton-screen evidence:** Viget (2017, n = 136) found the skeleton group *less* likely to agree content loaded quickly, with average perceived waits of 2.82 s (skeleton) vs 2.41 s (spinner) vs 2.29 s (blank). A 2018 ECCE paper (Mejtoft et al.) reportedly found skeletons rated faster, with small samples. Commentary concludes no study shows a large, reliable perceived-speed win for either pattern. [Weak] — [Viget: A Bone to Pick with Skeleton Screens](https://viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens); [DEV Community summary](https://dev.to/helloashish99/skeleton-screens-vs-spinners-perceived-load-time-1nlj); [AWS Builder Center](https://builder.aws.com/content/3M2CqltW6KlyigfOOolCKaohj0D/stop-treating-a-spinner-as-a-default-design-loading-feedback-by-how-long-the-wait-is)

### Inferences
- **Implication: acknowledge immediately, finish visibly.** After a folder drop, respond within ~100 ms (the drop zone changes to "Reading 14 files…"), then show the first frame of the most relevant clip as soon as it can be decoded, before telemetry parsing finishes. "People want to get started" means: let the user start playing while the rest indexes in the background.
- **Implication: use honest, specific progress for long work.** Parsing GPS from many MP4s or exporting a clip can take several seconds or more. Show a determinate bar with a count ("Reading GPS: 9 of 14 clips") rather than a spinner; Maister's "uncertain waits feel longer" and "unexplained waits feel longer" both apply. Do not show a fake percentage that jumps; a bar that slows near the end causes the "35 minutes instead of 30" feeling.
- **Implication: give users something useful to do while waiting** ("occupied time feels shorter"): let them scrub, view the map, or read "What happens next: mark the moment, then save a clip."
- **Implication: deal with the fear of being forgotten.** For an export over a few seconds, keep a visible progress item that remains after the user moves elsewhere in the app, and end with a clear completion state.
- **Implication: keep scrubbing and seeking fast.** For the enthusiast, the feeling of quality comes from immediate frame-accurate scrubbing (aim well below 100 ms for visual feedback while dragging, even if a full-quality frame arrives a little later, e.g., by showing a lower-resolution or nearest keyframe first). This is a behavioral-level quality signal (section 5).
- **Implication: do not choose skeletons expecting a measured speed gain.** Use them only where the layout is known (for example, the telemetry panel shape) and the wait is short; use determinate progress for long, countable work.

### Gaps
- Nielsen's response-time limits (0.1 s feels instant; 1 s keeps flow of thought; 10 s keeps attention; beyond that, show percent-done and allow interruption) are widely cited from his 1993 *Usability Engineering* and the NN/g article "Response Times: The 3 Important Limits", but the page could not be fetched in this session (nngroup.com was blocked), so the exact wording is unverified here: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/
- The original Doherty & Thadani paper and Maister's original text were not read directly; summaries are secondary.
- The Mejtoft et al. 2018 skeleton-screen result is known only from a secondary blog.
- Progress-bar research (e.g., Harrison et al. on accelerating progress bars feeling faster) was not retrieved and is not cited.

## 5. Peak-end rule, Zeigarnik, goal-gradient, control/agency, feedback, Norman's three levels

### Takeaway
Memory of an experience is weighted by its worst moment and its end (peak-end) [Strong], so the *end* of the incident flow (export and summary) disproportionately shapes how the tool is remembered. Goal-gradient (effort rises near a visible goal) is solid in field data [Moderate-Strong]. The Zeigarnik *memory* effect does not survive meta-analysis, though the urge to resume unfinished tasks (Ovsiankina) does [Strong, 2025 meta-analysis]. Norman's visceral/behavioral/reflective model is a useful framework for design, not an empirical law.

### Cited Findings
- **Peak-end rule:** Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less": people preferred a longer painful episode that ended less painfully. In Redelmeier & Kahneman's colonoscopy studies, patients rated discomfort every 60 s; later judgments were predicted by the worst and final moments, while duration (4–69 min) did not significantly matter. In a randomised follow-up, patients given a few extra minutes of milder discomfort at the end were significantly more likely to return for repeat colonoscopy. [Strong] — [Kahneman et al. 1993 PDF (UZH)](https://ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20(1993).pdf); [Wikipedia: Peak–end rule](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Peak_end_rule)
- **Goal-gradient (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, *Journal of Marketing Research* 43:39–58):** coffee-shop reward-card customers bought more often as they neared the free coffee; "illusionary goal progress" (e.g., a card with bonus stamps already filled) also sped completion. Builds on Hull (1934). [Moderate-Strong] — [Columbia Business School PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf); [Columbia research summary](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
- **Zeigarnik effect largely fails; Ovsiankina holds:** Ghibellini & Meier (2025), *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12: meta-analysis concluding the memory advantage for interrupted tasks "lacks universal validity" and has been hard to replicate, while the Ovsiankina effect (tendency to resume interrupted tasks) is a general tendency. A blog reports a ratio of interrupted to completed tasks recalled of 0.99 across 37 studies and ~67% resumption (unverified numbers). [Strong] — [Paper PDF, University of Bern](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf); [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Wikipedia: Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)
- **Sense of control:** Shneiderman's "support internal locus of control" and "permit easy reversal of actions" (see section 3). [Moderate] — [Interaction Design Foundation](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/shneiderman-s-eight-golden-rules-will-help-you-design-better-interfaces)
- **Norman's three levels (Emotional Design, 2004):** *visceral* (immediate, pre-conscious reaction to appearance), *behavioral* (pleasure and effectiveness of use: function, usability, feel), *reflective* (conscious meaning, self-image, the story the user tells afterwards). The levels act together, not in sequence, and reflective satisfaction can make users forgive visceral or behavioral flaws. [Framework; not an empirical law] — [Wikipedia: Emotional Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Design); [Norman, jnd.org](https://jnd.org/emotional-design/); [NN/g video: 3 levels of emotional processing](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/3-levels-emotional-processing/)

### Inferences
- **Implication (peak-end): design the end.** The worst moment for the incident user is probably the start (confusion over many files, or seeing the crash) and the end is the export. Make the end the high point: a clear completion screen — "Clip saved: front_rear_2026-11-24_14-32-01.mp4 (30 s). Original files not changed. Summary saved: incident_summary.pdf" — with "Show in folder" and "Save another angle". Avoid ending on a generic toast that disappears in 3 seconds.
- **Implication (peak-end): reduce the bad peak.** The first-time "pile of 200 unnamed MP4 files" moment is the likely low point; automatic grouping by trip and time, with events marked, softens it. Seeing the collision is emotionally hard: do not autoplay at full volume, start paused on the frame a few seconds before the event, and let the user choose to play.
- **Implication (goal-gradient): show progress toward "proof saved".** A light three- or four-step indicator (Find → Review → Save) gives the user a visible goal and a sense of momentum. Do not add fake "illusionary progress" to manipulate; but starting the user at step 2 when the app has already found the event is honest progress.
- **Implication (Zeigarnik vs. Ovsiankina): don't rely on "unfinished task" memory; support resuming.** If the user closes the tab mid-review, a local-only "Resume where you left off? (marked range 14:32:08–14:32:38)" offer uses the reliable resumption tendency. Store it only locally and say so; make it easy to clear (privacy).
- **Implication (control/agency):** the user, not the app, decides what counts as the incident: auto-detection proposes, the user confirms; overlays (speed, GPS) can be turned on and off in the export, with a preview of exactly what will be saved.
- **Implication (feedback loops):** every input gets a visible reaction: hovering the timeline shows a time preview; pressing a shortcut shows a short label ("Frame +1"); marking in/out shows the range on the timeline and map together, so the user sees cause and effect across views.
- **Implication (Norman's levels for DashLens):** *visceral* = calm, uncluttered, well-set type and video edge to edge; *behavioral* = instant scrubbing, frame stepping, keyboard control, synchronised map/telemetry; *reflective* = "I handled this well; I have solid proof; my data stayed private." The reflective level matters most for the incident user and is served by the completion screen and the privacy proof.

### Gaps
- No study found applying peak-end directly to software task flows with controlled measurement (the evidence is from medical and lab pain studies).
- The Ghibellini & Meier summary statistics (0.99 ratio, 67% resumption) come from a secondary blog and are unverified.
- Laws of UX entries on peak-end and goal-gradient were not fetched; the primary papers were used instead.

## 6. Delight: when it helps and when it is inappropriate; Kano; microcopy tone

### Takeaway
Delight only works on top of working, usable functionality (Walter's hierarchy; Kano: unmet basic needs cancel any "delighters") [Moderate, practitioner frameworks]. In a serious, stressful context, humour and playful animation in errors or the incident flow are inappropriate; delight should come from things working smoothly and surprisingly well ("deep delight"), not from jokes. Voice should stay steady while tone adapts to the user's emotional state (Mailchimp).

### Cited Findings
- **Hierarchy of user needs (Aarron Walter, via NN/g):** functionality → reliability → usability → pleasure/delight; higher needs can only be achieved after lower ones are met. NN/g's "theory of user delight" and "pillars of user delight" distinguish surface delight from deeper delight grounded in function and usability. [Moderate, practitioner] — [NN/g: A Theory of User Delight](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/theory-user-delight/); [NN/g: Pillars of User Delight](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/pillars-user-delight/); [NN/g video: Delightful UX is like a 3-legged stool](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/delightful-ux/)
- **Kano model:** satisfaction depends on feature class (basic, performance, delighters); if basic needs are not met, "the performance and delighters mean bupkis." [Moderate as a framework] — [Contentful blog on Kano and delight](https://contentful.com/blog/kano-model-delight-new-product-features)
- **Delight in errors:** Contentful's practitioner "delight scale" allows playful touches only in simple validations, never in critical or serious error messages, and warns that delight becomes annoying when repeated. [Weak/practitioner] — [Contentful blog](https://www.contentful.com/blog/2022/04/14/kano-model-delight-new-product-features/)
- **Emotional context:** UserTesting advises testing delight by asking participants to imagine something going wrong; they tend to report that humour and levity are not appreciated then. [Weak/practitioner] — [UserTesting: Why designing for delight doesn't always work](https://www.usertesting.com/blog/delight)
- **Mailchimp voice and tone:** voice is human, familiar, friendly and straightforward; voice stays steady while tone changes with the situation; "clarity over entertainment"; lead with the main point; when unsure of tone, keep a straight face. [Moderate, widely used practitioner standard] — [Mailchimp Content Style Guide: Voice and Tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/); [Mailchimp style guide TL;DR](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/tldr/)
- **GOV.UK:** no "sorry"/"please" in validation errors; say what happened and how to fix it (see section 3). [Moderate] — [GOV.UK Design System: Error message](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message)
- **Trauma-informed "hope" principle:** Chayn lists hope among its eight principles; messaging should leave the user with a way forward. [Practitioner] — [Catalyst/Promo Cymru](https://promo.cymru/resource/trauma-informed-design-an-introduction-for-non-profits)

### Inferences
- **Implication: two tone registers, one voice.** Incident flow: plain, calm, factual, no exclamation marks, no emoji, no jokes ("Event found at 14:32:11. Review it, then save a clip."). Enthusiast/idle contexts (empty state, about page, trip statistics): a little warmth is acceptable ("Longest drive this month: 2 h 14 min"). Never put playful copy in error, data-loss or export states.
- **Implication: "deep delight" for DashLens** = the app finds the event for you; front and rear cameras are synced automatically; the map follows the video; the export includes a neat summary sheet ready to email. These are Kano "delighters" that are also functional, so they are appropriate even in a crisis.
- **Implication: restrained motion.** Short, purposeful transitions (e.g., timeline zooming to the event) help orientation; decorative animation (confetti on export) would clash with the context and repeat poorly for enthusiasts. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **Implication: avoid false cheer and blame in copy.** "Great job!" after exporting crash footage is jarring. Prefer neutral confirmation plus next steps ("Saved. You can attach this file to your insurance claim."). Errors never blame the user or their camera ("Your camera saved this clip in a format…" → better "This clip uses a format DashLens can't read yet (H.265 in .TS)…").

### Gaps
- Atlassian's voice-and-tone guidance (e.g., "bold, optimistic, practical") was not surfaced by search, so it is not cited.
- No controlled study found measuring the effect of humour in error messages on stressed users; the guidance is practitioner consensus.
- NN/g delight articles were not fetched (blocked); their content here is from search summaries.

## 7. Colour psychology: evidence-based vs. myth

### Takeaway
A broad, correlational pattern exists: red, yellow and highly saturated colours are associated with high arousal; blue, green, white and grey with low arousal [Moderate, systematic review]. But specific behavioural effects (red hurts test performance, red increases attractiveness) have replicated poorly [Weak], and the field describes itself as nascent. For DashLens, base colour decisions mainly on contrast, conventions (red = danger/stop), colour-blind safety and redundancy with text/icons, not on claims about mood.

### Cited Findings
- **Elliot & Maier (2014) review, colour-in-context theory:** colour effects depend on context and learned associations; the authors describe the field as at a nascent stage. [Moderate, review] — [ResearchGate: Elliot & Maier, Annual Review of Psychology 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243965076_Color_Psychology_Effects_of_Perceiving_Color_on_Psychological_Functioning_in_Humans); [Elliot 2015, Frontiers in Psychology review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/pdf)
- **128-year systematic review (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2024):** more articles reported RED, YELLOW and SATURATED colours with high arousal, and GREEN, BLUE, WHITE and GREY with low arousal; this is a summary of correlations in the literature, not a controlled test. [Moderate] — [Springer: "Do we feel colours?"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-024-02615-z)
- **Red and intellectual performance fails to replicate:** four replication attempts of the "red word impairs performance" effect failed (the authors say this does not necessarily invalidate the idea of red effects in achievement settings); a meta-analysis concluded the evidence for a harmful effect of red in achievement contexts is not as clear as early research suggested. [Strong that the effect is weak or unreliable] — [Collabra: Four Replication Attempts](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/6/1/3/113047/Processing-the-Word-Red-and-Intellectual); [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01772-1)
- **Red-attractiveness effect less robust than claimed:** a re-examination found the red effect "probably is not as robust" as the original team's replications suggested. [Moderate] — [Springer: Men in red, a reexamination](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-015-0866-8)
- **Red and arousal:** a PeerJ study reports direct effects of red on arousal, with context-dependent effects on valence (pleasantness). [Moderate, single study] — [PeerJ 2515](https://peerj.com/articles/2515/)
- **"Blue calms" is mostly popular claim:** no dedicated replication of blue-reduces-stress effects was found; secondary sources often cite Palmer & Schloss (2010), which is about colour *preference* (ecological valence), not calming. [Weak] — [Vitamin of Colors blog, noting failed replications (commercial source)](https://vitaminofcolors.com/en/blog/the-psychology-of-colours/)

### Inferences
- **Implication: use red sparingly and only for meaning.** Since red/saturated colours are associated with arousal and red is a learned danger signal, keep red for real data problems (corrupt clip, export failed) and maybe the impact marker; don't make the whole incident UI red. A G-sensor spike can be marked with a clear shape and label ("Impact 2.4 g") in a strong but not alarming accent.
- **Implication: a calm neutral base.** A neutral, low-saturation interface (greys, a single restrained accent such as blue) is consistent with the low-arousal association and also lets the video dominate, which matters more. Justify it on contrast, focus and convention; don't claim it will "calm" users.
- **Implication: never use colour alone.** Event types, GPS quality and speed bands must also be shown with text, icons or patterns (colour-blind users; WCAG 1.4.1 use of colour). A speed trace coloured green→red should also have numbers and axis labels.
- **Implication: dark theme for video is a convention, not a psychology claim.** A dark surround reduces glare and makes video contrast clearer; offer both themes and follow the system setting.

### Gaps
- Could not locate a strong controlled study showing interface colour schemes change stress levels in real tasks.
- Palmer & Schloss (2010) was not retrieved; its characterisation above is from general knowledge and should be checked.
- Cross-cultural colour meaning (e.g., red as luck in China, white as mourning) was not researched in this session.
